## X-Ray Diffraction

![alt text](image-43.png)

> Now as I told you, there is an incident beam.  
> If nothing was happening and only some absorption of this x-ray and some transmission, then we would have said that there is no diffraction, just transmission.  
> So the very fact that you get a beam in a direction not expected in the direction of the transmitted beam that is what we are calling the diffracted beam.  
> Let us look at the X-ray diffraction in a little bit more detail.  

![alt text](image-44.png)

> So you have a periodic set of atoms in the crystal.  
> X-ray is coming and hitting and of course X-ray is not picking and choosing one particular atom, it is hitting all the atoms.  

![alt text](image-45.png)

![alt text](image-46.png)

> Let us focus on one atom and as the X-ray is coming, it will interact.  
> The electrons in the atom will interact here with the X-ray and will generate new X-ray beams travelling in all directions  
> So for a single atom or a single electron there is no concept of a diffracted beam.  
> Diffraction is in entire 3D space  
> Similarly all the atoms receive this X-ray  

![alt text](image-47.png)

> I have not shown this line for all the atom, but I'm showing this scattered line for all the atoms they are receiving and they are scattering.  

![alt text](image-48.png)

> Then you can pick and choose a certain direction , the blue direction here.  
> And now see whether the X-ray coming from this atom1 and atom2 in the blue direction they are reinforcing or not.  
> So you can apply the path difference and phase difference rule and see whether you get addition or you get cancelation.  
> If you have an addition then you have a strong beam, I mean waves get added up to give you strong beam  
> They could have canceled also. That depends upon the phase difference you know.  
> I mean, they are In-phase they will add  
> If they are out-of-phase they will subtract and so on.  
> If they were all in phase, you will get a strong beam and that is what we will then call the diffracted beam.  

> Now comes the Bragg's genius  

![alt text](image-49.png)

> This much was just a diffraction  
> This much what Laue also saw and got his nobel prize.  
> So Bragg had to do something new to get his Nobel prize.  
> So Bragg introduced this plane.  
> He said for this diffracted beam, I can draw this blue plane and think of it as a reflection.  
> That the black incoming x-ray is reflected into this blue X-ray by these mirror planes.  
> Totally hopothetical and nothing to do with the physics here. Physics has already been done. Physics has been done that you have all these waves and you got a diffracted beam. Analyze it. Laue did that.
> Laue's analysis did not require any introduction of mirror planes.  
> Simply addition of all the waves from all the atoms and you will get , you will predict the correct diffracted beam.  
> But then introducing this mirror plane by the bragg brought in enormous simplicity of the analysis of diffraction pattern  
> And this is why Bragg got nobel prize  

![alt text](image-50.png)

> Of course, the beam may not be adding up in this blue direction.  
> suppose this red, because each atom is giving beams in all the direction and so.  


![alt text](image-51.png)

> It may happen that in some other red direction also the beams add up and are in phase  
> But then it is not appearing to be mirror reflected from the blue  
> Don't worry Bragg said.  
> Consider now this. Switch off the blue plane consider red planes from which it will appear to be reflected  
> For this wall, this wass Bragg's genius.  
> This was imaginative gem. which didn't come from a logical deduction or something  
> But by seeing , looking at the problem and coming up with this nice mirror planes.
